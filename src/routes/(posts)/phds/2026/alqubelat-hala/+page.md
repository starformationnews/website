Protoplanetary discs are the natal environments of planetary systems. High-resolution sub-millimetre and infrared observations have revealed that approximately 10\% of protoplanetary discs host large central cavities ($\gtrsim 10\,\mathrm{au}$), often accompanied by complex substructures and inner-to-outer disc misalignments. While various mechanisms—including photoevaporative winds and embedded planetary or stellar companions—have been proposed to carve these cavities, the extent to which tight, unseen stellar and sub-stellar spectroscopic companions drive these architectures remains a fundamental open question. This thesis systematically tests the binary companion hypothesis by searching for close-in companions within a sample of substructured protoplanetary discs and evaluating their dynamical impact on the disc morphology and mass accretion.

This work leverages high-resolution, multi-epoch time-series spectroscopy obtained from ESPRESSO, UVES, and X-Shooter on the Very Large Telescope to measure radial velocity (RV) variations and absorption line-profile asymmetries across a target sample of six young stars hosting discs. For the eccentric double-lined spectroscopic binary (SB2) DQ Tau, high-precision RV monitoring over nine orbital cycles is used to derive refined orbital parameters, and disentangle component-resolved accretion tracers. For transition disc hosts (RY Lup, SY Cha, J1604, LkCa 15, and T Cha), multi-epoch RVs, line-bisector spans, and surface spot modelling are applied to disentangle genuine Keplerian motion from stellar activity-induced jitter, establishing quantitative detection limits on candidate companion parameter spaces.

For DQ Tau, we conclude that accretion dominance alternates between the primary and secondary stars near periastron passages over short timescales of a few orbital cycles, and report a prograde apsidal motion ($\Delta\omega \sim +30^\circ$ in the argument of periastron) driven by disc feedback or an external perturber.

For transition discs systems, the results demonstrate that close spectroscopic binaries are rare within the surveyed transition disc sample and that magnetic activity dominates the observed RV variability. For RY Lup, extreme surface spot coverage ($ff_{\mathrm{tot}} \sim 26\text{--}29\%$) and high activity indices ($R'_{\mathrm{HK}}$) produce strong RV jitter mimicking a close-in companion, showing that magnetic activity and orbital dynamics are deeply intertwined. For SY Cha and J1604, RV shifts correlate directly with line-profile asymmetry, leaving no periodic companion signals after activity subtraction. For T Cha and LkCa 15, parameter space mapping demonstrates that allowed close-in companions ($a \lesssim 0.1\,\mathrm{au}$) are located too far inward to dynamically hold open their large outer cavities ($\ge 30\text{--}40$ au) probed by millimetre interferometry even if the orbits were highly eccentric. However, close substellar companions could truncate the inner disc cavities probed by near-infrared (VLTI) interferometry.


These findings lead to the following conclusions regarding protoplanetary disc evolution:

(a) Close spectroscopic stellar or substellar binaries are not the primary driving mechanism responsible for the large (>10 au) central cavities and misalignments mapped by sub-millimetre interferometry across our sample of transition discs.

(b) The small inner cavities ($< 0.2$ au) observed in transition discs and probed by near-infrared interferometry are possibly carved by inner low-mass companions. 

(c) The dynamical influence of close-in binary companions remains decoupled from large-scale outer disc substructures probed by ALMA in our sample.

(d) Inner binary companions ($a < 0.1$ au) modulate accretion dynamics on shorter timescales than those predicted by the theory.

(e) Stellar magnetic activity poses the dominant observational bias in searching for young companions, frequently producing false-positive RV signals that require dense, multi-epoch, multi-wavelength monitoring to disentangle.

(f) The large outer disc cavities predominantly carved by other mechanisms such as photoevaporative winds or multiple non-detected, low-mass planetary systems operating at separations not probed by the RV technique used in this thesis.

RV surveys can constrain the maximum mass of these putative planetary mass systems located in the cavity. However, detecting and characterising the orbits of these companions will require extensive multi-wavelength monitoring in the near-infrared to reduce magnetic jitter, along with space-based astrometry of Gaia to track orbital accelerations, and high contrast imaging for direct detections.
